## Dative fresh-day check — BY VOICE (first ever spoken session)

Two surveys, 8 spoken answers.

### Round 1 (5 questions)
1. "Er gibt mir das Buch" → transcribed as `hier gibt Mia das Buch` — UNVERIFIED,
   see transcription note below
2. "Sie gibt dem Kind Wasser." ✅ gibt + das-word dative
3. "Ich gebe **ihn dem** Hund." ❌ → "ihr den Hund" — **cases inverted**
4. "Er spielt mit dem Kind." ✅ dative preposition
5. "Ich gebe **Ihnen den** Buch." ⚠️ used formal Ihnen for "you all"; den Buch wrong

### Round 2 (3 retries)
1. `hier gibt Mia das Buch` again — identical transcription, see below
2. "Ich gebe **ihr den** Hund." ✅ **corrected on the first retry**
3. "Ich gebe **euch** den Buch. Ein Buch. Ich gebe euch den Buch."
   — **euch produced for the first time** ✅ (listed since 2026-09-03, never
   used until now). But "den Buch" persisted, with an audible self-correction
   attempt that landed back on the wrong form.

## Findings

**1. Dative/accusative inversion — the core dative concept.** In a giving
sentence the PERSON is dative and the THING is accusative. He had them exactly
backwards, which is the classic beginner error. Resolved on one retry, but new
enough to need a fresh-day retest.

**2. "den" for das-words — now confirmed recurring, no longer light.** Third
instance (2026-09-02 das/den, 2026-09-04 "den Schuhe", today "den Buch" twice).
The self-correction mid-utterance is telling: the doubt is present, the
resolution isn't. He produces "ein Buch" correctly elsewhere, so this isn't a
gender-knowledge gap — "den" is being reached for as a generic accusative marker.

**3. geben — NOT resolved. Reopened, and the reason matters.** During the
session "gibt" appeared correct in every voice instance, and it was marked
resolved. Then the learner typed the one unverified sentence:
**"Er gebt mir das Buch"** — gebt is the ihr-form. Whisper had transcribed that
same spoken sentence as containing "gibt".

So either he said "gebt" and STT normalized it to the grammatical form, or he
says it correctly but writes it wrongly. Either way the error is live, and the
methodological point stands: **speech-to-text cannot be trusted to verify
fine morphology** — it is trained to output plausible text, which means it
repairs exactly the ending-level errors a grammar check is looking for.
Conjugation endings must be checked in writing from now on.

**4. Transcription limitation, both directions.** "Er gibt mir das Buch" came
back twice, identically, as "hier gibt Mia das Buch". Whisper is deterministic
enough that re-asking reproduces the same mishearing. Method note added to
VOICE_SETUP.md: when a reply looks phonetically garbled, ask for that one
sentence in TYPED form rather than re-asking by voice.

**5. Unprompted correct usage.** During the mic test he volunteered:
"Hallo, ich heiße Pavin. Ich komme aus Indien. Ich wohne in Würzburg. Ich esse
gern Brot." — four clean sentences including "gern + verb" used correctly, the
exact construction he got wrong in writing the day before. Also used kommen aus
and wohnen in, neither formally taught.

## Voice conversation — first real spoken practice

After the dative check, tuned the voice config and had an actual conversation.

### Config that works (learner-approved)
`voice="onyx"`, `tts_model="gpt-4o-mini-tts"` with tts_instructions telling it to
speak as a native German speaker. Learner's verdict on the accent and pace:
"this sounds really good and we will continue with this." **Kokoro is no longer
needed for the accent problem** — only for cost, if that becomes an issue.

Turn-taking: `vad_aggressiveness=0`, silence detection ON, but
**`listen_duration_min` must be about 10**, not 3. At 3 it cut him off
mid-sentence while he was still thinking ("Ich sehe die W."). At 10 it waits
through pauses and still ends naturally when he finishes.

### IMPORTANT format correction from the learner
I opened the conversation entirely in German. He stopped me:

> "If you speak like this, I don't understand half of the words... I want you to
> teach me mixed with English and German. You give the instructions in English
> and then what I should do in German, I mean, the corrections and so on."

He is right and this matches the tutor spec for beginner level. **English for
instructions and corrections, German for what he produces.** Saved to memory.

### Exchanges
1. "Ich esse Brot und sehe TV." — structure genuinely good, including correctly
   dropping the second "ich" in the und-clause, which is what German does.
   Only "TV" was an anglicism → taught **fernsehen** (separable, like aufstehen).
2. Retry: "Ich esse Brot und sehe fern." ✅ — transcribed as "Föhn" (hairdryer),
   another STT mishearing of correct German. Applied the separable pattern the
   moment it was explained.
3. "Morgen stehe ich auf sieben Uhr." — **verb-second perfect**, but the
   separable prefix "auf" landed after the subject instead of at the end, and
   "um" was dropped. Notable because this exact sentence shape was correct in
   WRITING on 09-01 and again in the 09-05 timed drill — it broke only under
   spoken real-time pressure. Voice is a new and heavier load source.
4. Retry: "Morgen stehe ich um sieben Uhr auf." ✅ clean.

### Takeaway
Voice is doing exactly what it was bought for: surfacing errors that only appear
under real-time pressure. Three separate things (dative case inversion, separable
prefix placement, the TV anglicism) showed up spoken that written practice had
not caught. All three corrected on the first retry.

## Written accusative check — 8/8, and it reclassified an error

Run in TEXT deliberately: accusative is an endings test, which is exactly what
speech-to-text cannot verify.

1. Ich sehe den Mann. ✅
2. Sie hat einen Hund. ✅
3. Ich esse einen Apfel. ✅
4. Wir sehen die Katze. ✅
5. **Er hat kein Buch.** ✅ ← probe: not "keinen"
6. Ich sehe ihn. ✅
7. **Ich sehe das Buch.** ✅ ← probe: not "den"
8. Sie hat keinen Hund. ✅

**8/8, no hesitation.** Questions 5 and 7 were planted specifically to test the
"den for das-words" error he had made TWICE by voice about an hour earlier
("Ich gebe euch den Buch"). In writing he gets both right without pausing.

### Consequence: the error was misdiagnosed and is now reclassified
Earlier today it was logged as CONFIRMED RECURRING and marked as needing "an
explicit pass" on the rule. That would have been wasted teaching. The written
result proves the rule is known cold — it fails only under spoken real-time
pressure. Reclassified as **load-dependent**, joining the 3rd-person conjugation
pattern, with the same prescription: exercise it under load, don't drill the rule.

This is the second time today that voice and writing disagreed, and it points at
a general principle worth holding on to: **voice reveals what degrades under
pressure; writing reveals what is actually known.** Neither alone is the truth.
