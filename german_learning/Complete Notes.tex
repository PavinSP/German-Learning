\section{PART I --- THE FOUNDATION}\label{part-i-the-foundation}

\subsection{1.1 The two verbs everything is built
on}\label{the-two-verbs-everything-is-built-on}

\textbf{sein} --- to be. Irregular; there is no pattern to derive, it
just has to be known cold.

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
ich \textbf{bin} & wir \textbf{sind} \\
du \textbf{bist} & ihr \textbf{seid} \\
er/sie/es \textbf{ist} & sie/Sie \textbf{sind} \\
\end{longtable}
}

\textbf{haben} --- to have. Regular except the du and er forms.

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
ich \textbf{habe} & wir \textbf{haben} \\
du \textbf{hast} & ihr \textbf{habt} \\
er/sie/es \textbf{hat} & sie/Sie \textbf{haben} \\
\end{longtable}
}

These two carry more weight than any other verbs in the language. Beyond
their own meanings, \textbf{haben} and \textbf{sein} are what build the
past tense later (Part VII), so time spent making them automatic pays
twice.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The slip to watch:} \emph{du} takes \textbf{hast} and
\textbf{bist} --- never the unchanged forms \emph{haben}/\emph{sein}.
``Was hast du?'', not ``Was haben du?''
\end{quote}

\subsection{1.2 Two ways to say how you feel --- never
mixed}\label{two-ways-to-say-how-you-feel-never-mixed}

German has two constructions for states like hunger and tiredness, and
they cannot be combined:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{sein + adjective} --- Ich \textbf{bin} hungrig. / Ich
  \textbf{bin} müde.
\item
  \textbf{haben + noun} --- Ich \textbf{habe} Hunger. / Ich
  \textbf{habe} Durst.
\end{itemize}

English ``I am hungry'' maps onto the first pattern, which makes it the
natural default. The second is equally correct but needs the noun form.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Wrong:} \emph{Ich habe hungrig} --- this mixes the two patterns.
Either \emph{bin hungrig} or \emph{habe Hunger}, never a hybrid.
\end{quote}

\subsection{1.3 Capitalization is grammar, not
decoration}\label{capitalization-is-grammar-not-decoration}

\textbf{Every German noun is capitalized}, wherever it appears in the
sentence: das Buch, die Katze, der Hunger, die Zeit.

But \textbf{infinitives and separable prefixes stay lowercase} ---
unless the word has genuinely been turned into a noun:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
lowercase (verb) & capitalized (noun) \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
Ich sehe \textbf{fern}. & das \textbf{Fernsehen} (television) \\
Ich kann \textbf{schwimmen}. & das \textbf{Schwimmen} (swimming, the
activity) \\
\end{longtable}
}

The distinction is real: \emph{fern} is a prefix doing verb work,
\emph{Fernsehen} is a thing. Same letters, different jobs.

\subsection{1.4 sie, sie, and Sie}\label{sie-sie-and-sie}

Three words, spelled almost identically, told apart by capitalization
and by the verb:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Form & Meaning & Verb \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
sie & she & sie \textbf{ist} \\
sie & they & sie \textbf{sind} \\
\textbf{Sie} \emph{(always capital)} & you (formal) & Sie
\textbf{sind} \\
\end{longtable}
}

Mid-sentence, the capital is the only visible difference:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  ``Die Frau ist glücklich, denn \textbf{sie} hat ein Buch.'' ---
  \emph{she}
\item
  ``\textbf{Sie} haben ein Buch.'' --- \emph{you}, formal
\end{itemize}

And \textbf{ihr} is never formal. It is informal plural ``you all'' only
--- for friends, family, a group of kids. Formal address is always
\textbf{Sie + sind}, whether you are talking to one person or twenty.

\subsection{2.2 What the cases actually
mark}\label{what-the-cases-actually-mark}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Nominative} --- the subject. Who or what is doing the verb.
\item
  \textbf{Accusative} --- the direct object. What the verb is done
  \emph{to}.
\item
  \textbf{Dative} --- the indirect object. Who \emph{receives} it.
\item
  \textbf{Genitive} --- possession. Not yet covered.
\end{enumerate}

Case is decided by \textbf{the noun's role in the sentence}. It is not a
property of the verb's type --- there is no such thing as an
``accusative verb'' versus a ``dative verb'' in general. haben, sehen
and geben all take accusative direct objects.

\subsection{2.3 When to use accusative}\label{when-to-use-accusative}

\textbf{Accusative marks the direct object of a verb.} Most ``doing''
verbs take one: haben, sehen, essen, trinken, kaufen, lesen, nehmen.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The test:} ask \emph{what is the verb doing something to?} A
clear answer means that noun is accusative.
\end{quote}

\textbf{The one big exception is sein.} ``To be'' doesn't act on
anything --- it equates two things --- so nouns after \emph{sein} stay
nominative:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Das ist \textbf{mein} Hund. \emph{(not meinen)}
\item
  Ist das \textbf{dein} Hund? \emph{(same reason --- still a
  sein-sentence)}
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Why this trips people:} ``Ist das deinen Hund?'' feels right
because \emph{Hund} looks like an object. But nothing is being done to
the dog; the sentence is just saying \emph{that = your dog}. No action,
no accusative.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Modal verbs change nothing here.} The infinitive at the end
decides the case, exactly as it would on its own:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Ich möchte \textbf{den Hund} sehen. --- \emph{sehen} takes accusative,
  so den Hund.
\item
  Ich kann schlafen. --- \emph{schlafen} takes no object at all.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.4 Dative arrives by two separate
routes}\label{dative-arrives-by-two-separate-routes}

This is worth separating clearly, because the endings look identical but
the reasons are unrelated.

\textbf{Route 1 --- the indirect object.} The receiver, usually a
person:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Ich gebe dem Mann ein Buch.} \emph{dem Mann} = dative (who
receives) · \emph{ein Buch} = accusative (what is given)
\end{quote}

\textbf{Route 2 --- certain prepositions simply demand it}, with no
object role involved at all:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Er spielt mit mir.} \emph{mir} isn't receiving anything.
\emph{mit} just forces dative. Full list in Part V.
\end{quote}

\subsection{2.5 Verbs that take dative and nothing
else}\label{verbs-that-take-dative-and-nothing-else}

A small group of verbs takes a \textbf{dative} object even though there
is only one object, and even though English makes it look direct:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Verb & Correct & Not \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
helfen --- to help & Ich helfe \textbf{ihr}. & \st{Ich helfe sie} \\
danken --- to thank & Ich danke \textbf{ihm}. & \st{Ich danke ihn} \\
gehören --- to belong to & Das Buch gehört \textbf{mir}. & \st{gehört
mich} \\
\end{longtable}
}

Also in this family: \emph{gefallen}, \emph{antworten}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Why it feels wrong:} ``I help her'' looks like a plain direct
object in English. German treats the helped person as a
\textbf{recipient} of help, not a thing being acted on. Once you hear it
as ``I give help \emph{to} her,'' the dative stops feeling arbitrary.

\textbf{The practical trap:} it is easy to spot that these verbs
\emph{need} dative and still reach for the accusative pronoun by reflex.
The forms you want are \textbf{mir, dir, ihm, ihr, uns, euch, ihnen} ---
never mich, dich, ihn, sie.
\end{quote}

\section{PART III --- PRONOUNS}\label{part-iii-pronouns}

\subsection{3.1 The full table}\label{the-full-table}

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Nominative & Accusative & Dative \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
ich --- I & \textbf{mich} & \textbf{mir} \\
du --- you & \textbf{dich} & \textbf{dir} \\
er --- he & \textbf{ihn} & \textbf{ihm} \\
sie --- she & sie & \textbf{ihr} \\
es --- it & es & \textbf{ihm} \\
wir --- we & \textbf{uns} & \textbf{uns} \\
ihr --- you all & \textbf{euch} & \textbf{euch} \\
sie --- they & sie & \textbf{ihnen} \\
Sie --- you (formal) & Sie & \textbf{Ihnen} \\
\end{longtable}
}

\textbf{Three things make this much smaller than it looks:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Only four dative forms are genuinely new} --- mir, dir, ihm,
  ihnen. \emph{uns} and \emph{euch} are identical in both object cases;
  es/sie/Sie carry over.
\item
  \textbf{er changes twice} --- er -\textgreater{} ihn -\textgreater{}
  ihm, mirroring der -\textgreater{} den -\textgreater{} dem exactly.
  The same shape, so if the articles are solid these follow.
\item
  \textbf{es and sie barely move.} Both hold still in the accusative.
  Only the dative shifts them --- and that is where the one real trap
  lives:
\end{enumerate}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{es -\textgreater{} ihm. sie -\textgreater{} ihr.}

\emph{es} shares its dative form with \emph{er}, which is
counter-intuitive enough that it is easy to over-apply and give
\textbf{ihm} for both es and sie. If you remember one thing from this
table, make it this line.
\end{quote}

\subsection{3.2 The three jobs of ``ihr''}\label{the-three-jobs-of-ihr}

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}
  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{(\linewidth - 4\tabcolsep) * \real{0.3333}}
  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{(\linewidth - 4\tabcolsep) * \real{0.3333}}
  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{(\linewidth - 4\tabcolsep) * \real{0.3333}}@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
\begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright
Form
\end{minipage} & \begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright
Job
\end{minipage} & \begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright

\midrule\noalign{}

\endlastfoot
ihr & ``you all'' --- subject & \textbf{Ihr} habt ein Buch \\

ihr / ihre & ``her'' --- possessive & \textbf{Ihre} Katze ist müde \\

Context resolves it, and usually the verb does the work: \emph{ihr habt}
can only be ``you all'', because a dative \emph{ihr} could never be the
subject of \emph{habt}.

\subsection{3.3 The pronoun trap worth real
attention}\label{the-pronoun-trap-worth-real-attention}

\textbf{A German pronoun follows the noun's grammatical gender --- never
the English meaning, never whether the thing is a person or an object.}

Three steps, every time:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Find the noun being replaced.
\item
  Recall its article --- der, die, or das.
\item
  Convert: der -\textgreater{} er/ihn · die -\textgreater{} sie/sie ·
  das -\textgreater{} es/es
\end{enumerate}

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Sentence & Becomes & Why \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
Ich sehe \textbf{den Tisch}. & Ich sehe \textbf{ihn}. & der Tisch \\
Ich habe \textbf{das Buch}. & Ich habe \textbf{es}. & das Buch \\
Sie kauft \textbf{die Milch}. & Sie kauft \textbf{sie}. & die Milch \\
Ich esse \textbf{einen Apfel}. & Ich esse \textbf{ihn}. & der Apfel \\
\end{longtable}
}

\textbf{Now the part that feels wrong and is correct anyway:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{der Apfel} is an object, but takes \textbf{ihn} --- literally
  ``I eat him.''
\item
  \textbf{das Kind} is a person, but takes \textbf{es} --- ``she sees
  it.''
\item
  \textbf{das Mädchen} (girl) is also a das-word -\textgreater{}
  \textbf{es}.
\end{itemize}

English picks \emph{it} or \emph{him/her} based on whether something is
a thing or a person. German does not make that distinction at all. When
a German pronoun sounds wrong to an English ear, that is expected --- it
is not a signal that you have made a mistake.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Self-check that makes this automatic:} before choosing a
pronoun, say the noun \emph{with its article} --- ``der Apfel''
-\textgreater{} der -\textgreater{} ihn.

This has a useful consequence: if you cannot recall a noun's article,
you cannot choose its pronoun. That is the strongest practical argument
for \textbf{always learning nouns together with der/die/das}. The
article isn't decoration; it is load-bearing information you will need
repeatedly.
\end{quote}

\subsection{3.4 Possessives use the same
endings}\label{possessives-use-the-same-endings}

  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{(\linewidth - 6\tabcolsep) * \real{0.2500}}
  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{(\linewidth - 6\tabcolsep) * \real{0.2500}}@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
\begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright
\end{minipage} & \begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright
der/das-word
\end{minipage} & \begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright
die-word
\end{minipage} & \begin{minipage}[b]{\linewidth}\raggedright

\midrule\noalign{}

Nominative & mein Vater · \textbf{unser} Auto & mein\textbf{e} Mutter &

Accusative & mein\textbf{en} Vater & mein\textbf{e} Mutter &

Identical pattern for dein-, sein-, ihr-, unser-, Ihr-.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The ending to be careful with: -en is DATIVE plural, not plural
in general.} This one is genuinely easy to over-generalise:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  mit \textbf{unseren} Eltern --- dative, because \emph{mit} forces it
  -\textgreater{} \textbf{-en}
\item
  gegen \textbf{unsere} Eltern --- accusative plural -\textgreater{}
  \textbf{no -en}
\end{itemize}

``Plural takes -en'' is a tempting rule and a false one. The plural
accusative leaves the possessive unmarked, exactly like the singular
die-word form.
\end{quote}

Note also that \textbf{neuter nominative stays bare}: \emph{unser Auto},
not \emph{unsere Auto} --- same as \emph{mein Buch}.

\subsection{3.5 uns versus unser-}\label{uns-versus-unser-}

Two different words that look related:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{uns} --- pronoun, ``us'': Er sieht \textbf{uns}.
\item
  \textbf{unser-} --- possessive, ``our'': \textbf{unsere} Eltern, mit
  \textbf{unseren} Eltern
\end{itemize}

Same shape of confusion as \emph{ihr} (you all) versus \emph{ihre}
(her). A pronoun and its look-alike possessive do different jobs and
take different endings.

\section{PART IV --- NEGATION}\label{part-iv-negation}

\subsection{4.1 Choosing between nicht and
kein}\label{choosing-between-nicht-and-kein}

\textbf{kein} negates a noun that would otherwise take \emph{ein/eine}
or no article at all --- something indefinite or general:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Ich habe \textbf{keinen} Hund. \emph{(versus ``einen Hund'')}
\item
  Ich habe \textbf{keine} Zeit. \emph{(Zeit normally takes no article)}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{nicht} negates everything else:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  a verb --- Ich arbeite \textbf{nicht}.
\item
  an adjective --- Ich bin \textbf{nicht} müde.
\item
  a \textbf{specific, definite} noun --- Ich sehe den Hund
  \textbf{nicht}.
\end{itemize}

\emph{kein} takes the same endings as \emph{ein}: keinen Hund, keine
Zeit, kein Buch.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The test:} could the word be replaced with ``no \_\_\_''
naturally, as a general thing? -\textgreater{} kein. Is it a verb, an
adjective, or a specific already-identified noun? -\textgreater{} nicht.
\end{quote}

\subsection{4.2 Why ``kein den Hund'' cannot
exist}\label{why-kein-den-hund-cannot-exist}

Because \textbf{kein and der/die/das are alternatives, not stackable}.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{kein replaces the article slot entirely.} In \emph{Ich habe
  keinen Hund} there is no separate \emph{ein} --- kein \emph{is} the
  article.
\item
  \textbf{nicht doesn't touch the article at all.} In \emph{Ich sehe den
  Hund nicht}, \emph{den} stays exactly as it was and nicht sits
  separately, negating the action.
\end{itemize}

``kein den Hund'' would put two articles on one noun.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{So the real question is always: what was there originally?} An
ein/eine (or nothing) -\textgreater{} kein replaces it. A der/die/das
-\textgreater{} keep it and add nicht.
\end{quote}

\subsection{4.3 Where nicht goes}\label{where-nicht-goes}

\textbf{nicht sits as late in the sentence as it can, but immediately
before whatever it negates} --- a verb, an infinitive, or a separable
prefix:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Ich sehe \textbf{nicht} fern.
\item
  Er kann \textbf{nicht} schwimmen.
\item
  Ich stehe um sieben Uhr \textbf{nicht} auf.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A definite object comes before nicht}, because the object isn't
what's being negated --- it is established information:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Right:} Ich sehe \textbf{den Hund nicht}.
\item
  \textbf{Right:} So kann er \textbf{den Hund nicht} sehen.
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
The logic is worth holding onto rather than memorising the order: you
aren't negating \emph{the dog}, you're negating \emph{the seeing}. So
nicht attaches to the verb, and the dog --- already known information
--- stays in front.

\textbf{A shorter version of the same idea:} nicht pushes as far right
as it can, and a definite object gets out of its way.
\end{quote}

\textbf{``Ich sehe nicht den Hund'' is not wrong --- it is a different
sentence.} Putting nicht \emph{before} the object makes it
\textbf{contrastive}: it sets up a correction, and sounds unfinished
without one.

\subsection{4.4 keine Zeit versus nicht viel
Zeit}\label{keine-zeit-versus-nicht-viel-zeit}

Not two ways of saying one thing:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Wir haben keine Zeit.} --- no time at all.
\item
  \textbf{Wir haben nicht viel Zeit.} --- some time exists, just not
  much.
\end{itemize}

The second one uses \emph{nicht} despite \emph{Zeit} being a noun,
because nicht is negating \textbf{viel} (the quantifier), not the noun.
That is consistent with the rule in 4.1, not an exception to it.

\subsection{5.1 Prepositions that always take

\textbf{aus · außer · bei · mit · nach · seit · von · zu}

Worth memorising as a rhythm --- \emph{``aus, außer, bei, mit, nach,
seit, von, zu''} --- because there is no logic to derive; membership in
this list is the whole rule. Whatever follows goes into dative,
regardless of its role:

\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot

seit \textbf{dem} Jahr & zu \textbf{meiner} Mutter \\

}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The habit to guard against: dropping the article.} \emph{von
Arbeit}, \emph{nach Wochenende}, \emph{von Schule} all feel acceptable
because English drops articles freely (``from work'', ``after the
weekend''). German doesn't --- a dative preposition keeps its article
every time.
\end{quote}

\subsection{5.2 Prepositions that always take

\item

\item
  Wir spielen \textbf{gegen unsere} Eltern.
\item
  Ich stehe \textbf{um} sieben Uhr auf.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{in · an · auf · über · unter · vor · hinter · neben · zwischen}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Ignore the list of nine words.} They all follow the one
question, so the question is the only thing worth memorising. The usual
difficulty isn't the concept --- it's applying it at speed while also
getting the noun's gender right (\emph{der Garten} -\textgreater{} dem
or den, never der). Both parts have to happen at once, which is why this
takes practice rather than understanding.
\end{quote}

\textbf{A boundary worth being explicit about:} \emph{nach} is never
used for moving into an enclosed space. Going into a room is \textbf{in
den Raum}, not ``nach dem Raum''. nach is for cities, countries,
directions --- and for ``after'' in time (below).

\subsection{5.4 nach versus zu}\label{nach-versus-zu}

Both can mean ``to''. They split on whether the destination takes an
article.

\textbf{nach} --- destinations with \textbf{no} article: - Cities and
countries: nach Berlin, nach München, nach Deutschland, nach Indien -
Directions: nach links, nach oben - \textbf{And separately, ``after'' in
time:} nach dem Wochenende, nach dem Termin

\textbf{zu} --- destinations that \textbf{do} take an article: - People:
zu meiner Mutter - Places: zur Schule, zum Bahnhof, zum Arzt, zur Post -
Events: zur Arbeit

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The test:} does the destination normally come with der/die/das?
No -\textgreater{} nach. Yes -\textgreater{} zu.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Contractions:} zu + dem -\textgreater{} \textbf{zum} · zu + der
-\textgreater{} \textbf{zur}

\textbf{But possessives never contract.} \emph{zu meiner Mutter} stays
spelled out --- ``zur Mutter'' would mean ``to \emph{the} mother'', a
different sentence. Contractions only ever happen with the bare article.

\textbf{Both can appear in one sentence doing different jobs:}

\begin{quote}
Wir gehen \textbf{nach dem Termin} \emph{(after --- time)} \textbf{zum
Arzt} \emph{(to --- destination)}.
\end{quote}

Time before place --- see Part VI.

\emph{Later complication, not needed yet: a few countries take an
article and use }in* instead --- in die Schweiz, in die USA.*

\subsection{5.5 aus versus von --- two kinds of
``from''}\label{aus-versus-von-two-kinds-of-from}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{aus} --- out of an enclosed space, or origin/nationality Ich
  komme \textbf{aus} Indien. · Ich komme \textbf{aus dem} Haus.
\item
  \textbf{von} --- coming from a place you were at Sie kommt \textbf{von
  der} Arbeit. · Er kommt \textbf{von der} Schule.
\end{itemize}

Returning from work or school is \emph{von}. Physically exiting a
building is \emph{aus}. The overlap is genuinely subtle and native
speakers lean on feel.

\subsection{5.6 Fixed phrases that ignore the
rules}\label{fixed-phrases-that-ignore-the-rules}

Some phrases are set expressions and don't decompose:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{zu Hause} --- ``at home''. Not \emph{zur Haus}, not \emph{im
  Haus}.
\item
  \textbf{an der Ecke} --- ``on the corner'' (location). \emph{an}, not
  \emph{in}.
\item
  \textbf{zur Ecke} --- ``to the corner'' (destination).
\end{itemize}

\section{PART VI --- WORD ORDER}\label{part-vi-word-order}

\subsection{6.1 Verb-second --- the
backbone}\label{verb-second-the-backbone}

\textbf{The conjugated verb occupies the second position} --- second
\emph{slot}, not second \emph{word}. Position one can hold an entire
phrase.

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Position 1 & Position 2 --- VERB & Rest \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
Ich & esse & heute einen Apfel \\
\textbf{Heute} & \textbf{esse} & \textbf{ich} einen Apfel \\
Am Wochenende & gehe & ich ins Kino \\
Weil ich müde bin, & bleibe & ich zu Hause \\
\end{longtable}
}

When something other than the subject takes position one, \textbf{the
subject moves behind the verb}. The verb never moves.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Fronting changes emphasis, not just order.} \emph{Mein Vater
arbeitet heute} foregrounds the father; \emph{Heute arbeitet mein Vater}
foregrounds the day. Both are correct --- they answer different
questions.
\end{quote}

\subsection{6.2 Yes/no questions --- verb
first}\label{yesno-questions-verb-first}

Verb and subject swap:

\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
Du bist müde. & \textbf{Bist du} müde? \\
Sie wohnt in Berlin. & \textbf{Wohnt sie} in Berlin? \\
Er kann schwimmen. & \textbf{Kann er} schwimmen? \\
Du hast Zeit. & \textbf{Hast du} Zeit? \\
\end{longtable}
}

\subsection{6.3 W-questions --- question word first, verb
second}\label{w-questions-question-word-first-verb-second}

\textbf{wer · was · wo · wohin · wann · warum · wie}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Wo ist} der Mann? · \textbf{Was hast} du? · \textbf{Wie geht's}
dir?
\end{quote}

\subsection{6.4 weil sends the verb to the end --- and the main clause
still needs
V2}\label{weil-sends-the-verb-to-the-end-and-the-main-clause-still-needs-v2}

Two rules apply at once here, which is what makes this construction
genuinely tricky.

\textbf{First:} \emph{weil} pushes its own clause's verb to the very
end.

\begin{quote}
Weil ich müde \textbf{bin}, \ldots{} · Weil es \textbf{regnet}, \ldots{}
\end{quote}

\textbf{Second:} the main clause that follows still needs verb-second
--- because the entire weil-clause counts as position one:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Weil es regnet, bleibe ich zu Hause.}

position 1 = the whole clause · position 2 = \emph{bleibe} · subject
third
\end{quote}

Getting one half right and the other wrong is the usual outcome. Both
have to hold simultaneously.

\textbf{denn also means ``because'' but behaves completely differently.}
It is a coordinating conjunction, like \emph{und/aber/oder}, so word
order stays normal in both halves:

\begin{quote}
Ich bleibe zu Hause, \textbf{denn ich bin} müde.
\end{quote}

\subsection{6.5 Time --- Manner --- Place}\label{time-manner-place}

After the verb, elements line up \textbf{time -\textgreater{} manner
-\textgreater{} place}:

\begin{quote}
Ich gehe \textbf{heute} \emph{(time)} \textbf{mit meiner Mutter}
\emph{(manner)} \textbf{nach Berlin} \emph{(place)}.
\end{quote}

Time words sit right after the conjugated verb: \emph{Ich muss
\textbf{heute} schlafen.}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{This is the reverse of English}, which tends to put place before
time (``I'm going to Berlin today''). It is why \emph{Wir gehen nach dem
Termin zum Arzt} puts the appointment before the doctor.
\end{quote}

\subsection{6.6 Separable verbs}\label{separable-verbs}

In an ordinary present-tense sentence, the \textbf{prefix detaches and
goes to the very end}:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Verb & In a sentence \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
aufstehen & Ich \textbf{stehe} um sieben Uhr \textbf{auf}. \\
anrufen & Ich \textbf{rufe} dich \textbf{an}. \\
fernsehen & Ich \textbf{sehe} gern \textbf{fern}. \\
\end{longtable}
}

\textbf{With a modal verb it does not split} --- the whole infinitive
goes to the end intact:

\begin{quote}
Ich muss um sieben Uhr \textbf{aufstehen}.
\end{quote}

\textbf{With nicht}, the negation sits immediately before the prefix:

\begin{quote}
Ich stehe um sieben Uhr \textbf{nicht auf}.
\end{quote}

\subsection{7.1 Modal verbs}\label{modal-verbs}

\subsection{7.3 Perfekt --- the past tense of
conversation}\label{perfekt-the-past-tense-of-conversation}

\textbf{Structure:} helper verb (\textbf{haben} or \textbf{sein},
conjugated, position two) + \textbf{past participle at the very end}.

\begin{quote}
Ich \textbf{habe} gestern Fußball \textbf{gespielt}. Ich \textbf{bin}
ins Kino \textbf{gegangen}.
\end{quote}

The shape should feel familiar --- it is the same ``conjugated verb
second, other verb thrown to the end'' pattern as modal verbs and
separable verbs.

\subsubsection{Choosing haben or sein}\label{choosing-haben-or-sein}

\textbf{sein} is used for: - \textbf{movement from one place to another}
--- gehen, kommen, fahren, fliegen, laufen - \textbf{change of state}
--- aufstehen, einschlafen, sterben, werden - \textbf{two exceptions to
memorise} --- \emph{sein} itself (ich bin gewesen) and \emph{bleiben}
(ich bin geblieben)

\textbf{haben} covers everything else --- which is the large majority.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The test:} does the verb describe moving between two places, or
a genuine change of state? -\textgreater{} sein. Otherwise
-\textgreater{} haben.

\textbf{Where intuition misleads:} \emph{stehen} feels physical, but
standing still isn't going anywhere -\textgreater{} \textbf{haben}.
\emph{spielen} is energetic but involves no destination -\textgreater{}
\textbf{haben}. Meanwhile \emph{bleiben} means staying put and takes
\textbf{sein} anyway, purely as an exception.
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Building the participle}\label{building-the-participle}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Regular (weak):} ge + stem + \textbf{t} machen -\textgreater{}
  ge\textbf{mach}t · spielen -\textgreater{} ge\textbf{spiel}t · kaufen
  -\textgreater{} ge\textbf{kauf}t
\item
  \textbf{Irregular (strong):} ge + (often altered) stem + \textbf{en}
  --- no rule, memorised per verb, exactly like English \emph{go
  -\textgreater{} went}.
\item
  \textbf{Separable verbs:} the \emph{ge-} goes \textbf{between prefix
  and stem} einschlafen -\textgreater{} \textbf{ein·ge·schlafen} ·
  aufstehen -\textgreater{} \textbf{auf·ge·standen}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Two systems that are often confused, and shouldn't
be}\label{two-systems-that-are-often-confused-and-shouldnt-be}

\textbf{Choosing haben/sein and spelling the participle are completely
independent questions.} There is no correlation whatsoever:

\subsection{7.5 leben versus wohnen}\label{leben-versus-wohnen}

\begin{itemize}

\item
  \textbf{wohnen} --- to reside, to have an address: Ich \textbf{wohne}
  in Würzburg.
\item
  \textbf{leben} --- to be alive, a way of life, or living \emph{with}
  people: Er \textbf{lebt} mit seiner Mutter.
\end{itemize}

An address question takes \emph{wohnen}. Living \emph{with} someone, or
life in general, takes \emph{leben}.

\subsection{7.6 mögen versus gern}\label{muxf6gen-versus-gern}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{mögen} pairs with a \textbf{noun} --- Ich \textbf{mag} Musik.
\item
  \textbf{gern} pairs with a \textbf{verb} --- Ich lese \textbf{gern}. ·
  Ich sehe \textbf{gern} fern.
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
``Ich mag kochen'' is the natural English-shaped guess and the wrong
one. To like \emph{doing} something, use \textbf{gern} + the verb:
\emph{Ich koche gern.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{7.7 geben --- the double-object
verb}\label{geben-the-double-object-verb}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Ich gebe dem Mann ein Buch.} \emph{dem Mann} dative (receiver) ·
\emph{ein Buch} accusative (thing)
\end{quote}

When both objects are nouns, \textbf{dative comes first}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Don't add \emph{zu}.} The dative already carries ``to'':
\emph{Er gibt \textbf{seiner Mutter} das Buch}, not ``gibt das Buch zu
seiner Mutter.''

Also worth keeping apart by sound: \textbf{geben} (to give) and
\textbf{gehen} (to go) are easy to swap when writing quickly.
\end{quote}

\subsection{9.3 Plausible rules that are simply
false}\label{plausible-rules-that-are-simply-false}

Four shortcuts that feel like they should work and don't:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{``Accusative is for action verbs, dative for non-action
  verbs.''} Case comes from the noun's \emph{role}, not the verb's
  character.
\item
  \textbf{``Plural always takes -en.''} Dative plural does. Accusative
  plural doesn't.
\item
  \textbf{``Irregular verbs use sein in the Perfekt.''} Entirely
  unrelated systems --- \emph{arbeiten} is regular and takes haben;
  \emph{bleiben} is irregular and takes sein.
\item
  \textbf{``nach is for nouns in infinitive form.''} Nouns don't have
  infinitives. The real split is article versus no article.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{9.4 Small forms that are easy to
lose}\label{small-forms-that-are-easy-to-lose}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item

  \textbf{der} Arbeit}
\item
  \textbf{The pronoun in a Sie-imperative} --- \emph{Kommen \textbf{Sie}

\item
  \textbf{The -e on a feminine possessive} --- \emph{Ihr\textbf{e}

\subsection{9.6 Where errors actually come
from}\label{where-errors-actually-come-from}

Worth knowing, because it changes how to practise: most mistakes here
are \textbf{not} knowledge gaps. The same forms that come out correctly
in a focused drill break under load --- while producing free sentences,
while juggling a new structure, while speaking in real time.

Two consequences:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Isolated drilling can be misleading.} Something that passes
  every isolated test can still fail in free production. Mixed practice,
  where attention is divided, is the more honest test.
\item
  \textbf{A fast flawed answer teaches more than a slow careful one.}
  Errors that only appear at speed are exactly the errors that appear in
  conversation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{10.2 Principles worth
following}\label{principles-worth-following}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Nothing counts as learned until it survives a night.} A clean
  first attempt measures comprehension, not retention.
\item
  \textbf{Two new grammar concepts a week.} Extra study time should buy
  depth, not more topics.
\item
  \textbf{Every new word gets used in a sentence the same day.}
  Recognition and production are different skills.
\item
  \textbf{Retrieval beats review.} Producing from memory beats
  re-reading, every time.
\item
  \textbf{Learn nouns with their articles, always.} Without the article
  you can't form the pronoun, the accusative, or the dative.
\end{itemize}

\section{QUICK REFERENCE}\label{quick-reference}

\textbf{Articles} der · den · dem --- die · die · der --- das · das ·
dem --- \emph{plural:} die · die · den +n

\textbf{Pronouns} ich--mich--mir · du--dich--dir · er--ihn--ihm ·
sie--sie--\textbf{ihr} · es--es--\textbf{ihm} wir--uns--uns ·
ihr--euch--euch · sie--sie--ihnen · Sie--Sie--Ihnen

\textbf{Prepositions} \emph{Dative:} aus, außer, bei, mit, nach, seit,
von, zu \emph{Accusative:} für, um, durch, gegen, ohne \textbf{(FUDGO)}
\emph{Two-way:} in, an, auf, über, unter, vor, hinter, neben, zwischen
-\textgreater{} \textbf{wohin?} = accusative · \textbf{wo?} = dative

\textbf{Contractions} ins · im · ans · am · zum · zur · vom ---
\textbf{never with a possessive}

\textbf{Word order} Verb second · yes/no questions verb first ·
\emph{weil} verb last · time -\textgreater{} manner -\textgreater{}
place · separable prefix to the end · \emph{nicht} immediately before
what it negates

\textbf{Perfekt} haben/sein + participle at the end \emph{sein} = motion
or change of state, plus sein and bleiben \emph{haben} = everything else
